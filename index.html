Who I Am

A Builder with Something to Prove

My name is Misias Wilson. I'm a university student based in Blantyre, Malawi who decided early on that ordinary was never going to be enough. From a young age, I was fascinated not just by money, but by how systems work — how businesses are built, how value is created, and how ordinary people with extraordinary vision can reshape the world around them.

Growing up in Blantyre, I watched opportunity pass through the cracks of a country still finding its footing. Rather than waiting for someone else to build what Malawi needs, I chose to become the person who builds it. I'm currently pursuing my university degree while simultaneously building, investing, and learning at a pace that most people won't attempt until years later.

I'm not building for quick wins. I'm building for legacy. Every business I start, every investment I make, every lesson I learn is a brick in a structure I intend to be standing long after I'm gone. I believe Africa's greatest era is not in its past — it's ahead of us — and I intend to be one of the people who makes that era real.

What Building a Startup as a Student Taught Me

Misias Wilson · 5 min read · 2025

I started building NyumbakuMalawi while I still had exams to sit and assignments to submit. Most people around me thought I was making my life harder than it needed to be. Maybe they were right. But in the months since I started, I've learned more about business, people, and myself than any lecture theatre has ever given me.

"The gap between idea and execution is where most people quietly give up. I discovered this was both the hardest and most revealing part of the process."

The idea is the easy part

Everyone has ideas. I had this one sitting in my head for months — a proper rental property platform for Malawi, something clean and reliable that didn't rely on Facebook groups and word-of-mouth. The idea felt obvious. What I didn't understand at the time was how far an obvious idea is from an actual product that people use.

Once I started building, the friction began immediately. Who would list their properties? How would I reach tenants? How do I handle landlords who are not tech-savvy? Questions I had never asked myself began stacking up. I learned that having an idea is a starting gun, not a plan.

You have to do things before you are ready

I launched before I was ready. The website was not perfect. The listings were few. The brand was rough. But waiting for perfect would have meant never starting, and never starting would have meant never learning what "ready" actually looks like in practice. The product taught me more in two weeks of being live than six months of planning ever did.

As a student, I had the advantage of having very little to lose. No salary to protect, no reputation that a failed product would destroy. That freedom is a resource most people do not recognise until it is gone. I used it.

Time management is a business skill

Balancing university with building a business taught me something I did not expect: constraints force creativity. When you only have three hours of productive time in a day, you stop doing things that do not matter. You stop overthinking and start executing. My academic performance did not collapse — it clarified. I became more deliberate about everything.

The feedback loop is everything

Talking to actual users — landlords, tenants, property managers — rewired how I thought about the product constantly. Features I was proud of turned out to be irrelevant. Problems I had not considered turned out to be critical. Real-world feedback is brutal and it is the most valuable thing you can get. No startup succeeds by guessing. You have to be in constant conversation with the market.

What I would tell every student considering building

Start while you are still in school. Not because it is easier — it is not — but because failure at this stage costs almost nothing and teaches almost everything. The student who tries to build a business and stumbles is already miles ahead of the graduate who waited for the perfect moment. The perfect moment was always right now.

Building NyumbakuMalawi did not just teach me about property listings and tech platforms. It taught me about resilience, communication, humility, and the relentless discipline of showing up even when nothing is working. Those lessons have no textbook. You have to earn them.

Lessons from Starting a Business with Limited Resources

Misias Wilson · 4 min read · 2025

Nobody hands you capital in Malawi. Nobody gives you the network, the tools, or the easy path. If you are going to build something here, you build it with what you have — and more often than not, what you have is less than you would like. This reality, as frustrating as it can be, turned out to be one of the most important teachers I have ever had.

"Scarcity of resources does not limit what you can build. It limits what you can waste. And in the long run, that discipline is worth more than any capital injection."

Constraint is a form of focus

When I started NyumbakuMalawi, I did not have a large budget. I had a laptop, a phone, and a clear problem I wanted to solve. What that meant was that every decision had to count. I could not afford to explore twelve different approaches simultaneously. I had to pick one, go deep on it, and evaluate honestly. That forced discipline built habits that I now carry into everything I do.

Entrepreneurs with too much capital early often build bloated products because they can. Entrepreneurs with almost none build lean products because they must. Lean turned out to be the right approach for a market like Malawi, where users are pragmatic and simplicity is a feature, not a compromise.

Resourcefulness is the real unfair advantage

I learned to build with free tools, open-source platforms, and community knowledge. I learned to trade time for money — putting in hours that others would pay for. I learned to ask for help in ways that respected people's time and created genuine value for them in return. These are not the lessons they teach in business school. They are the lessons the market teaches when you are operating with your back against the wall.

The money mindset starts before you have money

One of the most dangerous lies people tell themselves is that they will start being disciplined with money once they have more of it. That is backwards. The habits you build when you have little are the exact habits that determine whether you can manage a lot. Every time I had to decide whether to spend or save, reinvest or withdraw, I was training the instincts that will govern larger decisions later.

Build credibility before you build capital

In a resource-constrained environment, your reputation is your most liquid asset. People invest in people they trust before they invest in businesses. My early focus was on building a reputation as someone who actually executes — not someone who just talks about building. That credibility has opened more doors than any formal pitch ever has.

If you are starting with little, start anyway. The limitation is temporary. The character it builds is permanent.

Why Malawi Needs More Young Builders

Misias Wilson · 7 min read · 2025

Malawi is one of the least developed countries in the world by most economic indicators. But statistics are not destiny. Behind every number is a reality that can be changed — and the people who change economic realities are builders. Entrepreneurs. Young people who refuse to accept the world as they inherited it.

I want to make the case that the single most important thing that can happen for Malawi's future is not foreign aid, not government policy, and not another development report. It is young Malawians deciding to build.

"Every great economy was built by someone who looked at a gap and chose to fill it rather than wait for someone else to. Malawi has more gaps than most. That is not a curse. That is a continent of opportunity."

The talent is already here

I meet brilliant young Malawians constantly — people with sharp minds, genuine hunger, and extraordinary work ethic. The problem is not that Malawi lacks talented people. The problem is the environment has not consistently channelled that talent into building. We have been educated to look for jobs rather than to create them. We have been taught to survive rather than to build.

That conditioning is changing, slowly but visibly. The generation entering adulthood right now has watched the rest of the world build technology, companies, and wealth at a pace their parents never imagined. They know it is possible. They just need the permission — or more accurately, they need to give themselves the permission.

The opportunity cost of not building

Every year that talented young Malawians spend waiting for formal employment is a year of compound learning, compound network growth, and compound economic activity that does not happen. When you build a business, even a small one, you create jobs, generate tax revenue, solve problems, and train yourself in ways that make you more valuable to every future endeavour. When you wait, none of that happens.

Malawi cannot afford to waste its youth on waiting. The structural unemployment problem will not be solved by government hiring alone. It will be solved, slowly but surely, by young people building businesses that employ other young people.

The digital opportunity is real and it is now

Something has shifted in the last five years. The internet has reached more Malawians than ever before. Mobile money is mainstream. The barriers to building a technology-enabled business are lower than they have ever been in history. You can build a product today with tools that cost nothing, reach customers without expensive advertising, and process payments without a traditional bank account.

NyumbakuMalawi exists because of this reality. A rental platform that would have required enormous capital investment a decade ago can now be built and launched by a student. That is not a small thing. That is the entire game changing.

We need builders who stay

One of the most painful patterns in African development is the brain drain — the export of the continent's best minds to economies that benefit from them while the home countries are left behind. I understand why it happens. I understand the pull of better infrastructure, higher salaries, and more opportunity.

But I believe deeply that the greatest version of a Malawian life is built in Malawi, for Malawi. Not because opportunity abroad is not real, but because the opportunity cost here — the chance to be the person who helps build a country rather than just benefit from one that is already built — is an opportunity that will not exist forever. The window is now.

What I am doing about it

This is not an essay I am writing from a comfortable distance. I am building in Malawi right now, with Malawian problems and Malawian users. Every obstacle I face, every lesson I learn, every small win I celebrate is data that I intend to share with the next young Malawian who wants to build but does not know where to start.

I am committed not just to building my own ventures but to contributing to the culture of building in Malawi — through mentorship, through transparency about what this actually looks like, and eventually through structured support for other founders. The ecosystem does not build itself. Someone has to go first.

I chose to go first. I am hoping you will come with me.
